Objective: Impact of Missing Data on PDP Estimation

The objective here is to rigorously explore how missing data, specifically under MAR and MNAR conditions, influences the estimation of PDPs and, subsequently, the interpretability and reliability of these plots in reflecting the true relationship between features and the target variable in a predictive model.

Missing Data Mechanisms: MAR and MNAR

  • Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): The probability of missingness is the same for all observations. The missingness of data under MCAR does not depend on the data itself.
  • Missing At Random (MAR): The probability of missingness is the same within groups defined by the observed data. MAR allows the missingness to depend on observed data but not on the missing data.
  • Missing Not At Random (MNAR): The probability of missingness depends on the unobserved data. The missingness is related to the reasons that led to the data being missing.

For PDPs, the distinction between MAR and MNAR is crucial because it directly influences how we interpret the effects of missing data on the estimation of the relationship between features and the outcome.

Theoretical Basis

  1. PDP Under Complete Data: A PDP aims to show the effect of a single or a set of features on the predicted outcome, averaged over the joint distribution of the other features. Mathematically, if (X_s) is the set of features of interest and (X_c) the complement set, the PDP is defined as:

    [ PDP(X_s) = E_{X_c}[f(X_s, X_c)] ]

    where (f) is the predictive model.

  2. PDP Estimation with Missing Data: When data is missing, especially under MAR or MNAR, the joint distribution of (X_c) that is used to compute the expectation in the PDP is no longer accurately represented in the dataset. This distortion can lead to biased estimates of the PDP.

  3. Impact of MAR on PDP: Under MAR, the missingness can be modeled based on the observed data. For estimation, techniques like imputation or weighted sampling that account for the probability of observing certain patterns of data can be used to adjust the estimation of the PDP. This adjustment attempts to recreate the joint distribution of (X_c) as if the data were complete.

  4. Impact of MNAR on PDP: MNAR presents a greater challenge since the missingness depends on unobserved data. Without making additional assumptions or having external information about the missingness mechanism, it is difficult to correct for the bias in PDP estimation. Any attempt to estimate the PDP under MNAR conditions inherently involves speculation about the nature of the missing data.

Mathematical Exploration for MAR

To explore this mathematically, let’s consider a simplified model where we can express the probability of missingness for a feature (X_j) in (X_c) as a function of observed data. For MAR, this could be expressed as (P(M_j | X_{obs})), where (M_j) indicates missingness in feature (X_j) and (X_{obs}) represents observed features.

The corrected PDP estimation would then involve integrating over the distribution of (X_c), weighted by the inverse of the probability of missingness, to approximate the expectation under the complete data scenario. This can be formally represented as:

[ PDP_{MAR}(X_s) = _{X_c} dP(X_c) ]

Challenges and Considerations

  • Modeling Missingness: Accurately modeling the probability of missingness, especially under MAR, requires careful consideration and robust statistical methods.
  • Estimation Bias: Even with adjustments, the estimation of PDPs in the presence of missing data may still be biased if the assumptions about the missingness mechanism are incorrect or if the model for missingness is inadequately specified.
  • Complexity in MNAR: Addressing MNAR conditions theoretically and mathematically is more challenging due to the need for assumptions or external information about the nature of the missingness.

To develop the formula for estimating the Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) under the Missing At Random (MAR) condition in a more mathematical manner, let’s first clarify the notation and the setup. We’re dealing with a scenario where we want to adjust the PDP estimation to account for missing data, specifically when this missingness is assumed to be MAR.

Setting and Notation:

  • Let (f) be our predictive model.
  • (X_s) represents the subset of features for which we want to estimate the PDP.
  • (X_c) represents the complement subset of features, some of which may have missing values.
  • (M_j) is an indicator for missingness in feature (X_j) in (X_c), with (M_j = 1) if (X_j) is missing and (M_j = 0) otherwise.
  • (P(M_j | X_{obs})) is the probability of missingness for feature (X_j), conditional on the observed data (X_{obs}).
  • The goal is to estimate (PDP_{MAR}(X_s)), the PDP of (X_s) under MAR conditions.

Original PDP Estimation:

Under complete data, the PDP for feature(s) (X_s) is defined as:

[ PDP(X_s) = E_{X_c}[f(X_s, X_c)] ]

This expectation averages the effect of (X_s) on the prediction across all values of (X_c), holding (X_s) constant.

Adjusted PDP Estimation Under MAR:

To adjust for missingness under MAR, we aim to estimate (PDP(X_s)) as if the missing data were observed, leveraging the MAR assumption. We introduce a weight (w(X_c)) for each observation in the dataset that inversely adjusts for the likelihood of being observed given the missingness pattern. This weight is crucial for correcting the bias introduced by the missing data.

Given (P(M_j | X_{obs})) as the probability that the data is observed (not missing), the weight (w(X_c)) can be defined as the inverse of this probability, ensuring that observations with higher likelihoods of being missing are up-weighted in the estimation of the PDP:

[ w(X_c) = ]

The adjusted PDP estimation under MAR, (PDP_{MAR}(X_s)), then incorporates these weights:

[ PDP_{MAR}(X_s) = _{X_c} f(X_s, X_c) w(X_c) , dP(X_c) ]

However, this formula simplifies the complex nature of handling multiple features with missing values. In practice, (w(X_c)) needs to account for the multidimensional nature of (X_c) and the corresponding missingness patterns. For a simplified case where missingness is independent across features, the weight could be the product of the inverse probabilities for each feature:

[ w(X_c) = _{j X_c} ]

Implementation Note:

Implementing this approach requires:

  1. Estimating (P(M_j | X_{obs})): This involves modeling the missingness mechanism. For each feature (X_j) with missing values, a model (e.g., logistic regression) is fitted to predict the probability of missingness based on observed data.

  2. Calculating Weights: For each observation, calculate (w(X_c)) using the estimated probabilities from the missingness models.

  3. Estimating (PDP_{MAR}(X_s)): Compute the weighted average of (f(X_s, X_c)) over the dataset, using the calculated weights to adjust for missingness.

This adjusted approach to estimating PDPs under MAR allows for more accurate interpretations of feature importance and relationships in the presence of missing data, provided the MAR assumption holds and the missingness can be adequately modeled.

To compare the weighted PDP (accounting for missing data) against the unweighted PDP and the PDP from the completely observed data (without any missingness), we need to follow a structured approach. This involves calculating three sets of PDPs:

  1. Weighted PDP - Adjusts for missingness using weights derived from the probability of missingness.
  2. Unweighted PDP - Ignores missingness and does not use weights.
  3. PDP from Completely Observed Data - Uses only the subset of data without any missing values.

Below is an R code that demonstrates how to calculate and plot these three PDPs for comparison:

library(randomForest)
library(ggplot2)
library(dplyr)

# Assume 'imputed_data' is your dataset with imputed missing values for X2
# Assume 'data' contains the original data with a 'Weights' column for missingness weights
# Assume 'model' is your predictive model trained on 'imputed_data'

# Create a sequence of values for X1 over its range for plotting the PDP
X1_values <- seq(min(imputed_data$X1), max(imputed_data$X1), length.out = 100)

# Function to calculate PDP
calculate_pdp <- function(data, model, X1_values, weights = NULL) {
  pdp_data <- expand.grid(X1 = X1_values, X2 = unique(data$X2))
  if (!is.null(weights)) {
    pdp_data$Weights <- weights
  }
  pdp_data$Predicted_Y <- predict(model, newdata = pdp_data)
  
  if (is.null(weights)) {
    return(data.frame(X1 = X1_values, Predicted_Y = tapply(pdp_data$Predicted_Y, pdp_data$X1, mean)))
  } else {
    # Calculate weighted mean
    pdp_results <- aggregate(Predicted_Y ~ X1, data = pdp_data, FUN = function(x, w) weighted.mean(x, w, na.rm = TRUE), w = pdp_data$Weights)
    return(pdp_results)
  }
}

# Calculate the three PDPs
weighted_pdp <- calculate_pdp(imputed_data, model, X1_values, weights = data$Weights)
unweighted_pdp <- calculate_pdp(imputed_data, model, X1_values)
completely_observed_pdp <- calculate_pdp(data[!is.na(data$X2), ], model, X1_values)

# Combine the PDPs for plotting
weighted_pdp$Type <- "Weighted"
unweighted_pdp$Type <- "Unweighted"
completely_observed_pdp$Type <- "Completely Observed"
combined_pdp <- rbind(weighted_pdp, unweighted_pdp, completely_observed_pdp)

# Plot the PDPs
ggplot(combined_pdp, aes(x = X1, y = Predicted_Y, color = Type)) +
  geom_line() +
  labs(title = "Comparison of PDPs", x = "X1", y = "Predicted Y") +
  theme_minimal() +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("Weighted" = "blue", "Unweighted" = "red", "Completely Observed" = "green"))

Explanation:

  • Function calculate_pdp: This function calculates the PDP for a given dataset, model, and set of (X_1) values. It can adjust for weights if provided, allowing for both weighted and unweighted calculations.
  • Three PDP Calculations: We calculate the weighted PDP using the weights, the unweighted PDP without considering weights, and the PDP from the completely observed dataset (without missing (X_2) values).
  • Plotting: We combine these PDP datasets, label them accordingly, and plot them using ggplot2. This visualization enables a direct comparison of how missing data, and its treatment, affects the perceived relationship between (X_1) and the predicted outcome (Y).

This approach illustrates the potential impact of missing data on model interpretations and highlights the importance of appropriately handling missingness in your analyses.

Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) plots are a tool for interpreting what a machine learning model has learned about the relationship between a feature (or features) and the prediction. Unlike Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs), ALE plots focus on the local effects of features, accounting for the average changes within small intervals, thus avoiding the introduction of unlikely data combinations and reducing computational cost for high-dimensional data. They are particularly useful for identifying complex interactions and nonlinear relationships in your model’s predictions.

Here, I’ll guide you through creating a simple ALE plot for a single continuous feature from scratch using R. This example will be basic, illustrating the core concept of ALE plots. For a more comprehensive implementation, especially for categorical features or feature interactions, consider looking into existing libraries or a more detailed algorithmic approach.

Step 2: Implementing ALE Calculation for a Single Feature

For simplicity, we’ll focus on calculating ALE for X1. The steps are:

  1. Divide the range of X1 into intervals.
  2. For each interval, calculate the difference in predictions that results from changing X1 within that interval, keeping all other features constant.
  3. Accumulate these differences to get the ALE values.

Here’s how you could implement it:

# ALE calculation for X1
calculate_ale <- function(data, model, feature, n_intervals = 10) {
  # Create intervals
  breaks <- quantile(data[[feature]], probs = seq(0, 1, length.out = n_intervals + 1))
  ale_values <- numeric(n_intervals)
  
  # Calculate ALE
  for (i in seq_len(n_intervals)) {
    # Subset data for the current interval
    interval_data <- data[data[[feature]] >= breaks[i] & data[[feature]] < breaks[i + 1], ]
    if (nrow(interval_data) > 1) {
      # Perturb feature within the interval
      min_val <- min(interval_data[[feature]])
      max_val <- max(interval_data[[feature]])
      
      # Copy data to calculate differences
      perturbed_data_min <- interval_data
      perturbed_data_max <- interval_data
      perturbed_data_min[[feature]] <- min_val
      perturbed_data_max[[feature]] <- max_val
      
      # Calculate differences in predictions
      preds_min <- predict(model, newdata = perturbed_data_min)
      preds_max <- predict(model, newdata = perturbed_data_max)
      diff <- mean(preds_max - preds_min)
      
      # Accumulate ALE values
      ale_values[i] <- diff / (max_val - min_val)
    }
  }
  
  # Convert differences to accumulated values
  ale_values <- cumsum(ale_values) - mean(cumsum(ale_values)) # Centering ALE
  
  list(breaks = breaks, ale_values = ale_values)
}

# Calculate ALE for X1
ale_result <- calculate_ale(data, model, "X1", n_intervals = 10)

# Plotting the ALE plot for X1
library(ggplot2)
ggplot() +
  geom_line(data = data.frame(X1 = ale_result$breaks[-length(ale_result$breaks)], ALE = ale_result$ale_values), 
            aes(x = X1, y = ALE)) +
  labs(title = "ALE Plot for X1", x = "X1", y = "Accumulated Local Effect") +
  theme_minimal()

Understanding the Implementation

This implementation divides the range of X1 into quantiles, calculates the average effect of X1 changing within each of those intervals on the prediction, and then accumulates these effects. The resulting plot shows how changes in X1 locally affect the predictions, offering insight into the relationship modeled by your machine learning algorithm.
